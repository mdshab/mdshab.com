This website contains a dataset of events across three thousand years, and
one thread through it — *communication* — runs from the Phoenician alphabet
to the phone in your pocket. Laid end to end, the thread tells a story that
no single decade can: every communication technology in history has done
two things at once. It **collapsed a distance** and it **created a power**.
The interesting history is what each era did with the new power.

## The alphabet: distance collapses into learning

Around three thousand years ago, Phoenician traders needed bookkeeping that
didn't require a decade of scribal training. Their fix — a small set of
signs for sounds, later stretched over by Greek vowels — made writing
learnable in months instead of years. The distance that collapsed: between
the literate few and everyone. The power created: ordinary people could
leave records, and eventually arguments, in writing.

## Roads and relays: distance collapses into days

The Persian Royal Road moved a message across an empire in about a week —
Herodotus marveled at it — by spending imperial money on horses, stations
and standby riders. The Mongol yam later did the same across Eurasia. The
power created: *centralized speed*. Whoever ran the relay knew things
before the provinces did. Every statebuilt network since, fiber included,
inherits this politics.

## Print: distance collapses into identical copies

Movable type in Korea, then Germany, made copies cheap and — more
important — *identical*. Identical text meant identical errors could be
corrected everywhere at once; science became cumulative; Reformation became
possible. The power created: publics. A distributed audience that shares
the same words is a new kind of political object.

## The wire: distance collapses into hours, then seconds

The telegraph (1844) is the moment communication *separated from
transportation*. Before it, information moved at the speed of whatever
carried it — horse, ship, train. After it, information outran everything.
Markets synchronized, militaries centralized, newspapers reorganized. The
transatlantic cable made it planetary. The telephone then made it intimate:
the network entered the hallway of the house.

## Packets: distance collapses into zero (allegedly)

TCP/IP made networks of networks speak one protocol; the web made writing
on them universal. Voice over IP — the technology I built a career on —
collapsed the last aristocracy of the network: voice stopped being a
physical circuit and became data like any other. The power created by the
internet age is still being negotiated: universal publishing, universal
surveillance, in the same wire.

## The pattern

Every jump on the thread reads, in its own century, as liberation followed
by consolidation. The alphabet escapes the scribes; empires standardize it.
Print escapes the church; states license it. The telegraph escapes the
horse; monopolies own the wire. The internet escapes the broadcasters — and
then platforms recentralize attention. The technology collapses the
distance; the institutions regrow the power; the culture renegotiates.

Which is why an infrastructure career feels, to me, like participating in
the thread rather than merely observing it. Somewhere between a SIP trunk
and a control plane, the same two questions from three thousand years ago
are still open: *who gets to speak*, and *who owns the road the words
travel on*.
