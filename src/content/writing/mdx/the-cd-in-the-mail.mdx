There is an envelope in my memory that has outlasted most of the hardware I
have owned. It came from the Netherlands, and inside was a pressed CD with
Ubuntu Linux on it — mailed for free, to a teenager who had filled in a form
on the other side of the world.

## The waiting is part of the story

The download was impossible: the connection of my early computing years
couldn't carry an operating system. So the open-source world did something
that sounds quaint now — it used the postal service. I ordered, and then I
*waited*. Weeks. When the envelope arrived, installing it was an event, not
a download.

It is hard to explain to a generation with mirrors-in-every-pocket what
that waiting did to the object. The CD was not a file. It was a *gift* from
strangers who had no reason to know I existed.

## The command line as a room

Windows 95 and Norton Commander had already taught me to move through a
computer by keyboard. Ubuntu turned that habit into a home. The terminal
was not a step backward from the GUI — it was a place where the system
*explained itself* if you asked correctly. Man pages, error messages,
configuration files in plain text: the machine became legible.

Legibility is the real gift open source gave me. Not the price — the
*readability*. Proprietary systems of that era were rooms with locked
drawers. Linux was a room where every drawer opened, and the sum of my
career is what I found in them.

## What I'd tell the kid with the envelope

The stack you're standing on will be replaced five times — the distro, the
job, the industry vocabulary, all of it. Don't cling to any layer. Cling to
what the envelope proved: that the interesting people are out there, that
the tools can be free in both senses, and that the distance between a
curious person and a working system is shorter than it looks. Sometimes it
is exactly one postal service wide.
