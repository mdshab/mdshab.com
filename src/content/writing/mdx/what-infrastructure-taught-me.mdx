Between 2007 and now, every platform I learned was replaced by another:
PBX-era VoIP gave way to unified communications, racks gave way to
virtualization, virtualization to cloud, cloud to cloud native. Each move
promised to make the previous one obsolete. What actually became obsolete
was the *tooling*. The lessons compounded. Here are the ones that survived
every shift.

## Reproduce, isolate, verify

From years on support desks and NOC floors: almost every outage mystery
yields to the same discipline. Can you reproduce it? Can you isolate the
variable? Can you verify the fix did what you think? Sophisticated tooling
changed the surface of this work, never its core.

## The abstraction ladder is real — and expensive to skip

Each rung — physical, virtualization, cloud, cloud native — hides the rung
below. That hiding is the product. But when something breaks *across* rungs,
the person who can still reason about the layer below diagnoses in minutes
what others chase for days. Abstractions are for building; every rung is for
debugging.

## Most "technical problems" are interface problems

Between teams, between systems, between on-call rotations. The hardest
incidents I've been part of were hard not because the technology was
exotic but because ownership was ambiguous. The fix was usually a sentence:
*this is yours until we say otherwise*.

## Reliability is a form of respect

Users stake their work on your platform. Every 9 of availability is a claim
about how much you value their time. This isn't sentimentality — it's the
product requirement that outlives every feature.

## Write it down

Runbooks, postmortems, decision records. The infrastructure that outlives
its builders is made of documents, not servers. Every team I've seen
level up did so by writing more, not by buying more.

## The long view is practical, not decorative

Knowing that the telegraph preceded the telephone, that packet switching
took a decade to matter, that "the cloud" is the third business model for
compute in fifty years — this changes how you react to hype cycles. The
technologies that matter look boring in year one and indispensable in year
ten. Patience is an engineering skill.
