The room I worked in as a VoIP administrator had a sound. Not the phones —
the **PBX cabinets**. Fans, disks, the deep hum of hardware that had been
running since before I was hired. When people say "the phone system," they
used to mean that room.

Today the equivalent is a dashboard and an API. This essay is about what
changed, what didn't, and why the second list matters more.

## What actually changed

**Where the intelligence lives.** A PBX concentrated everything: call
control, voicemail, IVR, billing records. A SIP trunk plus a cloud PBX
splits those jobs across systems owned by different people. Integration —
once a cable — became a *contract*.

**Where failure lives.** When the cabinet died, the failure was in the room.
When a cloud telephony integration misbehaves, the failure can be in your
config, the carrier's routing, a DNS record, or a certificate that expired
at 2 a.m. on a long weekend. Failure didn't get rarer; it got *distributed*.

**Where the leverage is.** Provisioning a new extension once meant a ticket
and a technician. Now it's an API call. When configuration became
programmable, everything downstream of configuration became automatable.

## What didn't change

**Dial tone is a promise.** Users don't care about your architecture. They
pick up a phone and expect a tone, and their call is expected to connect.
That expectation was old when the PBX was new, and it survives every
platform shift since. Whatever you build, the promise is identical.

**Capacity is seasonal.** Call volumes spike at the same moments business
spikes. The cloud made elasticity a purchase instead of a warehouse, but the
discipline of *planning for the busy hour* is the same discipline — the math
just runs in a different ledger.

**Voice is unforgiving.** Data can retry. A dropped word is gone. Jitter
budgets, codec choices and QoS are the cloud era's version of a clean copper
pair: invisible until wrong.

## How this fits into my career

I entered telecommunications in 2007 through technical support at Tel4Tel,
an international carrier, then moved into administration and supervision.
At FCP, the scope grew from NOC and network work into telecom platforms,
Linux systems and infrastructure services. Voice was one specialization
within that broader path.

The connection to cloud product work is practical. A managed service still
needs an underlying system, people who can operate it and a clear account
of what the customer can expect. Knowing the earlier layers helps me ask
better questions about the abstraction we offer today.
